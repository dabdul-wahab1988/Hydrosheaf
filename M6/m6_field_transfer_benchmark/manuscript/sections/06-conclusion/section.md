## Conclusion

Transferring an identifiability-aware sparse inverse reaction-inference workflow, together with its frozen reliability calibration, onto three real Ghanaian aquifer datasets showed that it degrades gracefully, honestly and largely reproducibly as field evidence is withdrawn, but not without limit. Dataset readiness varied sharply and predictably with tracer coverage and charge-balance quality across the three settings. Within the best-resourced dataset, seasonal and region-stratified transfer was stable, with every well reported conservatively as partially identifiable rather than over-resolved. Diagnostic robustness testing identified strontium and silica, not isotopes or fluoride, as the specific measurements whose removal drove the largest, sharpest loss of identifiability, while numerical fit quality remained essentially unchanged throughout, showing that fit alone cannot be trusted to reveal this loss. Connectivity assumptions changed which process network was inferred without changing whether individual point-to-point reactions were themselves resolvable. External transfer to two genuinely sparse datasets showed substantially higher non-identifiability than matched-tier Northern Ghana references, with the same strontium/silica mechanism responsible for the most severe case. Across all datasets, silicate and carbonate weathering proved harder to resolve without corroborating tracers than majors-corroborable processes such as nitrate loading, redox transformation and cation exchange. Hydrosheaf is therefore useful, in this application, as a conservative, evidence-gated field-transfer framework that states which process interpretations survive sparse data, which collapse, and which single measurement would most reduce ambiguity at a given site - and it is not, and does not claim to be, a substitute for independent validation of true reactions, flow paths or groundwater ages in these aquifers.
