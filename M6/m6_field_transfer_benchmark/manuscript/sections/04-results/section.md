## Results

### Dataset readiness and missingness

[[TAB:TAB-1]]

[[TAB:TAB-2]]

[[FIG:FIG-1]]

[[FIGREF:FIG-1]] and [[TABREF:TAB-1]] establish the evidence ceiling that bounds every subsequent result. Only Northern Ghana reached the maximum M6 evidence tier (Tier 4); Lower Anayari was capped at Tier 2 by the absence of strontium and silica, and Talensi was capped at Tier 1 by the additional absence of fluoride ([[TABREF:TAB-2]]). An independent charge-balance audit, applied identically to all three datasets, classified Northern Ghana as 294 quantitative, 19 screening and 7 exploratory of 320 samples (median absolute charge-balance error 1.5%); no external quality-class label exists in the canonical source data to check this classification against. Lower Anayari returned 36 quantitative, 5 screening and 0 exploratory samples of 41 (median 3.1%). Talensi returned 0 quantitative, 5 screening and 58 exploratory samples of 63 (median approximately 30%), identifying it as a genuinely low-quality-control external set from the outset, before any reaction inference was attempted. Missingness was not monotonic with overall evidence tier: iron, for example, was recorded in both external datasets but absent from Northern Ghana, so evidence completeness had to be tracked per variable rather than assumed from a single dataset-level tier label.

Applied identically to the same candidate-edge sets used in the connectivity experiment below, a supplementary sensitivity screen for competing, non-flow explanations of sample similarity returned high scores throughout (Supplementary Fig. S11, Supplementary Table S9): chemistry-similarity edges scored a mean null score above 0.89 in every dataset, and 96-100% of edges in every dataset/edge-set combination exceeded the 0.5 competing-explanation screening threshold. Geographic-nearest edges scored only marginally lower than chemistry-similarity edges. Because chemical similarity is, by construction, also how the reaction-inference pipeline itself selects a dominant family, this result was treated as a caveat rather than a contradiction: it indicates that a constructed edge should not, by itself, be read as evidence of a physical flow connection, reinforcing rather than weakening the honesty boundary applied throughout this study.

### Northern Ghana seasonal and region-stratified transfer

[[FIG:FIG-3]]

At full evidence (Tier 4), every one of the 160 Northern Ghana wells was classified partially identifiable, never identifiable and never non-identifiable ([[FIGREF:FIG-3]]), reproducing the conservative, class-level outcome previously found under the antecedent synthetic transfer. Mean MRS ranged narrowly from 70.6 (Upper West) to 71.2 (Northern Region), and mean bootstrap support stability from 0.965 to 0.976 across the four administrative regions (no independent aquifer-type classification exists for these boreholes), indicating that this conservative call was numerically stable and homogeneous rather than borderline or region-specific. The dominant Cl-corrected seasonal process was silicate weathering (114 of 160 wells), followed by redox transformation (18), cation exchange (16), agro-domestic nitrate loading (5), carbonate weathering (4) and evaporite dissolution (3), and silicate weathering was the modal process in every one of the four regions. No prior process-label concordance is reported: the only such reference available before this correction was a different, antecedent study's own inferred labels for the same boreholes, not independent ground truth, and it has been removed from this study rather than retained as a descriptive comparison (Methods).

### Robustness to removing diagnostics

[[TAB:TAB-3]]

[[FIG:FIG-4]]

Tier ablation produced the study's pivotal result ([[FIGREF:FIG-4]], [[TABREF:TAB-3]]). Descending from Tier 4 to Tier 3 (removing only the saturation-index lifter) left identifiability essentially unchanged (0% to 0.6% non-identifiable; mean MRS 71.0 to 70.7). Descending one further step, from Tier 3 to Tier 2 (removing strontium and silica), sent 51.9% of wells to non-identifiable while mean MRS barely moved (70.7 to 70.7). Removing fluoride (Tier 1) and then isotopes and all remaining lifters (Tier 0) left the non-identifiable fraction close to this level (51.9% and 60.0% respectively) and lowered mean score only modestly (to 68.6 and 68.4). Strontium and silica were therefore the load-bearing corroborating measurements for resolving silicate weathering, and their removal was the single largest driver of identifiability loss observed anywhere in this study. Reaction-family labels, in contrast, changed relative to the Tier 4 reference for a constant 35.6% of wells at every ablated tier from Tier 3 down to Tier 0, showing that family-level attribution and identifiability-class collapse are governed by different features of the evidence and do not move together. Across every tier, mean MRS changed by at most 2.6 points while the non-identifiable fraction moved by up to 51 percentage points between Tier 3 and Tier 2, and by up to 60 percentage points across the full Tier 4 to Tier 0 range, so numerical fit quality alone would not have revealed this loss of information.

These tier-ablation figures use within-well seasonal wet-to-dry residuals as the inference unit throughout, which matters for comparison with the external-transfer results below: the Northern Ghana reference values reported there are instead built from chemistry-similarity edges among dry-season samples only, a different inference-unit construction that is not directly comparable tier-for-tier to the seasonal-pair figures above, and the two should not be read as reproducing one another at nominally matching tiers.

### Edge and flow-path uncertainty

Comparing three Hydrosheaf-generated candidate edge-construction strategies against each other showed that inferred process-*network* composition was sensitive to the assumed connectivity: total variation distance from the chemistry-similarity reference set was 0.12 for geographic-nearest edges and 0.05 for the random/perturbed null set (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S5). Per-edge identifiability, however, was effectively edge-invariant: 99.3-100% of edges were classified partially identifiable and mean MRS ranged only 72.6-73.3 across all three edge sets. Connectivity assumptions therefore affected which process network was inferred at the network level, but not whether a given point-to-point reaction inference was resolvable, isolating exactly where an uncertain flow-path assumption does and does not matter.

### External transfer to Talensi and Lower Anayari

[[TAB:TAB-4]]

![Extended Data Fig. 1. Mean Mechanism Resolution Score, identifiability and dominant-process distributions for Talensi and Lower Anayari against matched-tier Northern Ghana references.](figures/extended_data/figureED1_external_transfer.png)

**Extended Data Fig. 1.** Mean Mechanism Resolution Score, identifiability and dominant-process distributions for Talensi and Lower Anayari against matched-tier Northern Ghana references. It quantifies the cost of missing diagnostics without treating prior labels as field truth.

Talensi and Lower Anayari measure complete pH, temperature and major-ion panels, so Hydrosheaf's own PHREEQC saturation-index computation was applied to them exactly as to Northern Ghana, rather than left uncomputed on the assumption that it required strontium or silica; saturation indices need only pH, temperature and major ions, so their availability is not gated behind the Sr/SiO2 lifter (Methods). Applying the identical pipeline at each dataset's native tier, now with saturation indices available as an additional corroborating lifter throughout, showed that external transfer no longer degraded uniformly relative to Northern Ghana evaluated at matched tiers with the same chemistry-similarity-edge construction and the same saturation-index lifter (Extended Data Fig. 1, [[TABREF:TAB-4]]; see the note above on why these edge-based Northern Ghana reference values differ from the seasonal-pair tier-ablation figures reported earlier, which do not use this ad hoc external-transfer tier construction). Talensi (Tier 1) returned 37.2% non-identifiable edges, materially *lower* than a Northern Ghana edge-based Tier-1 reference of 54.2%, despite Talensi additionally carrying a severe charge-balance imbalance that Northern Ghana does not; the direction of this comparison reverses what a data-availability-only account would predict, and is read here as evidence that per-dataset process composition (Talensi's edges were rarely carbonate-dominant, so saturation-index corroboration was rarely the limiting evidence) drives the non-identifiable rate at least as much as the evidence tier itself. Both edge-based Tier-1 figures remain below Northern Ghana's own seasonal-pair Tier 1 non-identifiable fraction (51.9%, reported above, which uses only isotopes as a lifter at that tier), underscoring again that the two inference-unit constructions are not directly comparable. Lower Anayari (Tier 2) returned 95.3% non-identifiable edges against a Northern Ghana Tier-2 reference of 53.3%: the great majority of Lower Anayari edges were silicate-dominant, a family that saturation indices do not corroborate (only carbonate weathering is gated by a calcite/dolomite saturation-index check in this framework), so the newly available saturation-index lifter had little opportunity to help. The single actionable implication is that strontium and silica remain the next-best measurement for Lower Anayari specifically, since silicate weathering is corroborated only by those tracers, not by saturation indices, in the evidence-lift gate used throughout this study.

### Within-campaign seasonal hold-forward

[[TAB:TAB-5]]

[[FIG:FIG-5]]

A truth-free, within-campaign one-step hold-forward benchmark tested whether wet-to-dry chemistry change was predictable at all, independently of the reaction-inference results above, using a fixed, disclosed arbitrary well-revelation order in place of a real sampling-date sequence that does not exist in the canonical field data ([[FIGREF:FIG-5]], [[TABREF:TAB-5]]). The more informative comparison, between the HydroSheaf graph-ridge predictor and a simpler expanding wet-to-dry mean-delta baseline that uses no connectivity information, was inconclusive: graph-ridge's mean absolute error (0.169 log1p units) was only marginally lower than the mean-delta baseline's (0.174), a small and not clearly resolved difference (paired difference -0.005, 95% CI [-0.012, 0.002], an interval spanning zero), so the specific incremental value of the processed connectivity graph over a simple seasonal baseline is not established by this test. Both predictors substantially outperformed persistence (0.342 mean absolute error; paired difference for graph-ridge, -0.173, 95% CI [-0.188, -0.158]), but persistence is a weak baseline (assuming no seasonal change at all) and besting it establishes only that within-campaign wet-season chemistry is materially more predictable than a no-change assumption, not that the graph-based approach specifically adds value. Empirical 90% predictive-interval coverage was close to nominal for both the graph-ridge (0.923) and mean-delta (0.903) predictors and well below nominal for persistence (0.631).

### Limitation map and process-class identifiability

[[FIG:FIG-6]]

Synthesising all five preceding experiments into a single per-process, per-dataset identifiability map showed that silicate weathering was non-identifiable in both external datasets but partially identifiable in Northern Ghana, since strontium and silica, the tracers that corroborate it, remain absent from both Talensi and Lower Anayari. Carbonate weathering, in contrast, was partially identifiable in all three datasets: the small number of carbonate-dominant edges in Talensi and Lower Anayari were corroborated by the saturation indices now computed independently for every dataset (Methods), which are not gated behind strontium/silica availability the way silicate corroboration is. Majors-corroborable processes (nitrate loading, redox transformation, cation exchange) remained partially identifiable everywhere ([[FIGREF:FIG-6]]). Conservative, class-level reporting admitted 7.1 alternative reactions on average per well, compared with 5.4 reactions that a single best-fit point estimate would have committed to on the same data, quantifying the additional alternatives that conservative reporting keeps visible rather than discarding.

### Validation and robustness diagnostics

![Extended Data Fig. 2. Ground-truth reaction recovery by archetype and evidence panel for 360 forward-simulated wells with known reactions and extents.](figures/extended_data/figureED2_synthetic_validation.png)

![Extended Data Fig. 3. Field non-identifiable fraction with the evidence-lift gate on versus off, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, alongside gate-independent structural diagnostics and transport-correction sensitivity.](figures/extended_data/figureED3_circularity_sensitivity.png)

**Extended Data Fig. 3.** Field non-identifiable fraction with the evidence-lift gate on versus off, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, alongside gate-independent structural diagnostics and transport-correction sensitivity. Retained as an explicit anti-circularity diagnostic.

Three supporting analyses tested whether the tier-ablation result above was an artefact of the evidence-lift gate rather than a real information effect. First, repeating the tier ablation with the gate switched off showed that the field data alone, without the gate, never returned a non-identifiable classification at any tier (0% throughout; Extended Data Fig. 3), while a gate-independent structural diagnostic on an extended reaction-basis model showed matrix rank rising from 8 to 11 and silicate-versus-non-silicate coherence falling from 0.707 to 0.500 as strontium and silica were added, confirming that the direction of the tier effect is real even though its reported magnitude is a property of the conservative gate. Second, a synthetic-well validation with known reaction truth (Extended Data Fig. 2) showed that adding strontium and silica genuinely improved recovery where theory predicts (cation-exchange family accuracy 0.45 to 0.66; carbonate and silicate family accuracy approximately 1.00 and 0.98 respectively), while exact-mineral-level recovery remained limited at every tier (F1 approximately 0.49-0.74), which is the quantitative justification for reporting at the class/family level throughout this study; the same synthetic wells spanned the full range of true identifiability class (35% identifiable, 30% partially identifiable, 23% non-identifiable, 12% equivalence-class). Because this is a synthetic discrimination check rather than field truth, it shows that the classifier can express all four classes but does not establish that Northern Ghana is hydrogeochemically homogeneous or that its analytical quality is uniformly high. Third, comparing three transport-correction treatments showed that omitting a correction altogether was degenerate (93% of wells collapsed to a spurious evapoconcentration signal), indicating that some transport correction is necessary. The two principled corrections, chloride-conservative and per-region recharge-endmember, agreed on the dominant reactive family for only 64.4% of wells, meaning more than a third of wells received a different dominant-family call depending on which correction was used; both corrections nonetheless identified an overall silicate-weathering-dominated network at the aggregate level (71% and 70% of wells respectively), so the network-level qualitative picture is similar across the two treatments even though well-level attribution is not, and this well-level sensitivity to the correction choice should be read as a genuine, only partially resolved source of uncertainty rather than as evidence of full correction-choice invariance. A targeted diagnosis of the Talensi charge imbalance (median -29%, a persistent 2.1-2.12 meq/L anion excess dominated by bicarbonate) showed that reassigning alkalinity from bicarbonate to calcium-carbonate units worsened rather than improved the balance, ruling out a unit-reporting error. The remaining explanations include an unmeasured cation pool or cation under-recovery, but the available data cannot identify the cause or attribute it specifically to artisanal-mining activity.
