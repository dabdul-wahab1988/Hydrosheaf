# Field transfer of Hydrosheaf under data scarcity: robustness, uncertainty and limits across three Ghanaian groundwater datasets

Groundwater process interpretation across semi-arid West Africa often relies on uneven field evidence. We tested whether a previously benchmarked, identifiability-aware sparse inverse reaction workflow, with calibration fixed, transfers to one well-resourced and two sparse Ghanaian groundwater datasets on a five-level evidence-tier ladder. At full evidence, all 160 Northern Ghana wells were conservatively classed as partially identifiable. Removing strontium and silica sent 52% to non-identifiable under a predeclared operational evidence gate, while fit quality barely changed. Lower Anayari, which lacks those tracers, returned 95.3% non-identifiable candidate edges. Talensi failed charge-balance screening and is therefore reported only as an exploratory screening case. Flow-path assumptions changed the inferred process network but not point identifiability. Wet-to-dry chemistry was predictable beyond no change, but not clearly beyond a simple seasonal baseline. The framework identifies which interpretations survive sparse evidence and where an additional measurement may reduce ambiguity; it does not validate true reactions, flow paths, or ages.

## Introduction

Groundwater is the dominant potable source across semi-arid West Africa, and its chemical evolution underpins decisions on drinking-water safety, irrigation suitability and vulnerability to geogenic fluoride mobilisation and agro-domestic nitrate loading [@macdonald2012africa]. Weathering of silicate and carbonate minerals, cation exchange, evapoconcentration, geogenic fluoride mobilisation and agro-domestic nitrate loading are the recurrent processes that set groundwater composition in the region [@anku2008ghana; @banoengyakubo2011ghana]. Yet the field datasets available to interpret these processes are chronically uneven: tracer coverage varies by well, sampling is frequently single-season, lithological and hydrogeological metadata are often absent, and analytical quality control is rarely stated explicitly. Process interpretation is therefore usually attempted with whatever evidence happens to be available, rather than with an explicit account of what that evidence can and cannot support.

Conventional inverse hydrogeochemical methods were developed for comparatively data-rich settings. Mass-balance and reaction-path modelling [@plummer1980massbalance] and computational tools such as NETPATH [@plummer1994netpath] and PHREEQC's inverse-modelling module [@parkhurst2013phreeqc] assume adequate analytical coverage of the ions and tracers needed to constrain a reaction system, and typically return a single best-fit narrative rather than a statement of which alternative reaction sets remain equally consistent with the data. This is a specific instance of the broader equifinality problem long recognised in environmental modelling, whereby multiple structurally different explanations can fit the same limited observations equally well [@beven2001glue; @beven2006manifesto]. Equifinality is worst exactly where data are thinnest, so the settings in which African groundwater interpretation is most needed are also the settings in which conventional single-fit reporting is least trustworthy.

A method validated only on a synthetic or laboratory benchmark demonstrates internal consistency, not field usefulness. By analogy with the wider hydrological-sciences literature on prediction in ungauged and data-limited basins, a research programme concerned primarily with streamflow prediction rather than reaction inference, a model's value there is argued to lie in how it degrades as evidence is withdrawn, not only in its best-case skill [@sivapalan2003pub; @hrachowitz2013pub]. The relevant question for an identifiability-aware hydrogeochemical workflow, previously validated under a controlled synthetic inverse benchmark, is therefore not whether it recovers a single correct answer in any one Ghanaian aquifer, but whether it degrades gracefully, honestly and reproducibly as diagnostic evidence, seasonal coverage and assumed connectivity are varied or withdrawn.

To the authors' knowledge, no existing Ghanaian or, more broadly, West African groundwater study, including the regional hydrochemistry studies drawn on for the field settings analysed here [@anku2008ghana; @banoengyakubo2011ghana; @chegbeleh2020talensi; @zakaria2021anayari], has reported this kind of transparent, quantitative map: which process interpretations remain stable under real, uneven field evidence, which collapse when a specific diagnostic is missing, and which single additional measurement would most reduce ambiguity at a given site. Those regional studies characterise dominant processes competently but do not quantify how sensitive that characterisation is to the evidence actually available, nor do they distinguish a genuinely well-constrained inference from one that merely fits well numerically. The practical stakes of this distinction are not abstract: whether a well's chemistry is attributed with confidence to silicate weathering, cation exchange or a geogenic trace-element source shapes whether it is flagged for further water-quality testing, prioritised for treatment, or left unmonitored, so an interpretation that looks equally confident whether or not it is actually well supported carries a real decision cost.

This is the general problem for which the specific evidence-tier structure of the three datasets analysed here is well suited. Northern Ghana, Talensi and Lower Anayari were not chosen because they are unusually data-rich or unusually sparse; they were chosen because, between them, they span the range of tracer coverage, seasonal repetition and quality-control transparency that recurs across published African groundwater datasets, making the transfer results reported here indicative of a broader class of settings rather than of these three sites alone.

This study addressed that gap by transferring a previously benchmarked, identifiability-aware sparse inverse reaction-inference workflow, together with its frozen reliability calibration, onto three real Ghanaian aquifer datasets spanning a deliberate range of evidence completeness, and by quantifying the stability of its process inferences, evidence gates and identifiability classes under six controlled stress tests, whose design and primary metrics were fixed in a locked analysis plan before the tests were executed. The aim was to evaluate how the workflow transfers to Ghanaian data-limited aquifer settings by quantifying the stability of process inference, evidence gates, reaction-class interpretation and uncertainty under seasonal variation, missing diagnostics, uncertain flow-path structure and transfer to genuinely sparse external datasets. Six corresponding objectives structured the analysis: characterising dataset readiness under an explicit evidence-tier ladder; quantifying seasonal and regional transfer stability within the best-resourced dataset; quantifying identifiability loss as diagnostics are withdrawn; quantifying sensitivity to assumed sample connectivity; quantifying the additional uncertainty incurred when the workflow is transferred to two genuinely sparse external datasets; and synthesising the preceding five objectives into a single per-process, per-dataset map of what is identifiable, weakly identifiable, or non-identifiable under field data scarcity.

The contribution is not a new inference method: the sparse inverse reaction model and its calibration are reused unchanged from the antecedent synthetic benchmark. The contribution is a conservative, evidence-gated account of what a data-limited field application of that method can and cannot support, replacing an implicit assumption of adequate coverage with a measured survival hierarchy, and identifying, for each dataset, the specific additional measurement that would most reduce ambiguity. This reframes equifinality under scarcity from an obstacle to be hidden by a confident-looking fit into an explicit, actionable output.

## Study design and datasets

### Ghanaian aquifer settings

Three real Ghanaian groundwater datasets were selected to span a deliberate range of hydrogeological setting and evidence completeness rather than to maximise sample size alone. The Northern Ghana dataset comprised 320 seasonal samples from 160 wells sampled in both the wet and dry seasons of March and August 2025, distributed across four administrative regions (Northern, North East, Upper East and Upper West) spanning the Voltaian sedimentary and Birimian/granitoid basement terrain typical of the area [@anku2008ghana; @banoengyakubo2011ghana]; no independent aquifer-type or lithology classification is available for these boreholes, so this study does not stratify Northern Ghana by lithological setting. The region shares the semi-arid-to-sub-humid seasonal rainfall regime typical of northern Ghana, in which a short, intense wet season and an extended dry season drive strong within-well seasonal concentration change, motivating the paired wet/dry sampling design used here. The Talensi dataset (63 samples) was drawn from an artisanal-small-scale-gold-mining-affected basement catchment in the Upper East Region, where silicate and carbonate weathering are reported as the main controls on groundwater chemistry alongside nitrate and trace-metal contamination linked to agrochemicals and wastewater [@chegbeleh2020talensi]. The Lower Anayari dataset (41 samples, referred to as "manu") was drawn from a shallow regolith/alluvial catchment in the same region where fluoride was among several factors, alongside sulphate, bicarbonate and nitrate, associated with reduced water-quality-index scores in a minority of samples; the great majority of samples in that catchment were classed as good quality, and the source study does not establish a geogenic origin for the fluoride specifically [@zakaria2021anayari]. All three settings are representative, in different ways, of the basement and regolith/alluvial hydrogeology that supplies most rural and peri-urban groundwater across northern Ghana, and none was selected because it was expected to give a particularly favourable or unfavourable result.

### Variable availability and evidence tiers

The three datasets differ systematically, not incidentally, in which diagnostic evidence they carry. Northern Ghana carries the fullest evidence stack used by this study: major ions, nitrate, fluoride, strontium, silica, and the stable isotopes delta18O and delta2H, all measured; calcite/dolomite/gypsum/halite/fluorite saturation indices are computed independently from this same measured chemistry via PHREEQC rather than supplied by the source workbook. No independent aquifer-type, geology-group, lithology, land-use, quality-control-class, or process-label metadata exists for these boreholes beyond region and district, and no directed graph of well connectivity is available; where the study reports a well-connectivity network, it is generated internally from chemical similarity, geographic proximity, or a randomised control, never imported. Northern Ghana was also sampled twice, in March (dry season) and August (wet season) 2025, giving a paired within-well seasonal design absent from the other two datasets. Lower Anayari carries major ions, nitrate, fluoride and the two stable isotopes, but no strontium, silica, or seasonal repeat sampling. Talensi carries major ions, nitrate, iron, redox potential and the two stable isotopes, but no fluoride, strontium, silica, seasonal repeat sampling or stated quality control. Both external datasets measure complete pH, temperature and major-ion panels, so the same independent PHREEQC saturation-index computation applied to Northern Ghana was also applied to them; saturation indices require only pH, temperature and major ions, not strontium or silica, so their absence from the tier ladder below reflects the ladder's own nested construction rather than a data ceiling, and they are used as an additional evidence-lift-gate lifter for both external datasets throughout (Methods). This structure was formalised as a five-level evidence-tier ladder, from Tier 0 (major ions only) to Tier 4 (majors, fluoride, isotopes, strontium/silica and saturation indices), against which each dataset's native ion-panel ceiling was fixed by what it actually contains: Tier 4 for Northern Ghana, Tier 2 for Lower Anayari, and Tier 1 for Talensi. The ladder is the experimental spine of the study: it is walked downward within Northern Ghana to quantify diagnostic value, and it is the reason the two external datasets cannot be treated as like-for-like replicates of Northern Ghana at a lower sample size (Figure 1).

### Why these datasets represent realistic data-limited conditions

These three datasets were treated as representative of, rather than exceptions to, the conditions under which African groundwater chemistry is typically interpreted: uneven tracer coverage across otherwise similar wells, single-campaign sampling for two of the three datasets, absent lithological and hydrogeological metadata for all three datasets at the individual-well level, and charge-balance quality that had to be recomputed independently rather than assumed, because no external quality-class label exists for any of the three datasets. An independent charge-balance audit, applied identically to all three datasets, confirmed this unevenness quantitatively and is reported as part of the Results.

A further boundary is definitional rather than empirical and is stated here because it governs every subsequent claim. An earlier stage of this study read a Northern Ghana workbook that additionally carried `Dominant_Process`, `Aquifer_Evolution_Label` and candidate `Graph_Edges` fields; those fields were themselves the output of a different, antecedent, non-independent analysis of the same boreholes, not measured or externally validated quantities, and reusing them would have imported that other study's own classifications and connectivity graph into what is meant to be an independent transfer test. They have therefore been removed from this study entirely rather than retained as a concordance reference: no claim in this study compares Hydrosheaf's inferred process labels or edge sets against them. Likewise, "Tier 4" denotes only the highest tier of the chemistry/isotope reaction-diagnostic ladder actually available in this study; it does not denote a complete, independently observed age-head-screen hydrogeological record, and no claim in this study should be read as asserting one.

## Hydrosheaf transfer workflow

### Datasets and harmonisation

Three real Ghanaian groundwater datasets were used: a Northern Ghana workbook of 320 seasonal samples from 160 wells (wet and dry campaigns, March and August 2025) spanning four administrative regions of the Voltaian sedimentary and Birimian/granitoid basement terrain typical of the area [@anku2008ghana; @banoengyakubo2011ghana]; an artisanal-mining-affected Talensi set of 63 samples [@chegbeleh2020talensi]; and a Lower Anayari (manu) set of 41 samples from a shallow regolith/alluvial catchment with documented geogenic fluoride occurrence [@zakaria2021anayari]. All ion concentrations were converted from mg/L to mmol/L by molar mass, isotope fields were read directly from the source worksheets, and Talensi longitudes were converted from a positive degrees-west convention to signed WGS84 values. No independent aquifer-type, geology-group or lithology classification exists for any of the three datasets at the individual-well level.

### Charge-balance screening and evidence tiers

An independent charge-balance error (CBE) was computed in meq/L from the harmonised ions and used to classify every sample as quantitative (|CBE| <=5%), screening (5-10%) or exploratory (>10%) [@fritz1994charge]. This classified Northern Ghana as 294 quantitative, 19 screening and 7 exploratory of 320 samples; primary analyses used the 294 quantitative records. No external quality-class label exists in the source data to check this classification against. A five-level evidence-tier ladder (Tier 0, major ions only, through Tier 4, majors plus fluoride, stable isotopes, strontium, silica and saturation indices) fixed the reaction-basis ion panel available to each dataset. Saturation indices for calcite, dolomite, gypsum, halite and fluorite were computed independently by live PHREEQC from each borehole or sample's own measured pH, temperature and major-ion chemistry, not supplied by any source workbook or file; because this computation needs only pH, temperature and major ions, it was applied identically to Northern Ghana, Talensi and Lower Anayari and used as an evidence-lift-gate lifter for all three, rather than withheld from the two external datasets on the assumption that it required strontium or silica. Native ion-panel tier ceilings were Tier 4 for Northern Ghana, Tier 2 for Lower Anayari and Tier 1 for Talensi, reflecting genuine absence of strontium, silica and seasonal repeat sampling from the two external datasets; saturation-index availability does not track this ion-panel ceiling and is reported separately.

### Inverse reaction inference

Each inference unit was a Cl-conservative-transport-corrected reactive residual: within-well wet-to-dry seasonal change for Northern Ghana, or the concentration difference along a candidate directed edge for the edge-based and external-transfer experiments. The conservative correction removed the concentration change attributable to evapoconcentration or dilution using chloride as a conservative tracer [@christophersen1992emma]:

<!-- EQ:EQ-1 -->
$$
r_i = x_{i}^{\text{target}} - f \, x_{i}^{\text{source}}, \qquad
f = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{x_{\text{Cl}}^{\text{target}}}{x_{\text{Cl}}^{\text{source}}},\, 0.2,\, 5.0\right)
$$

where $r_i$ is the reactive residual for ion $i$ and $f$ the estimated transport factor. The residual vector $\mathbf{r}$ was decomposed into non-negative, thermodynamically bounded reaction extents $\mathbf{z}$ using the frozen Milestone 5 (M5) sparse-regression estimator over a 16-reaction dictionary spanning silicate and carbonate weathering, cation exchange, evaporite dissolution, fluoride mobilisation, redox and nitrate loading [@tibshirani1996lasso; @zou2005elasticnet]:

<!-- EQ:EQ-2 -->
$$
\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{z} \,\in\, \mathcal{Z}}
\; \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{r} \rVert_2^2
+ \lambda_1 \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_1
+ \lambda_2 \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_2^2
$$

with $\mathbf{A}$ the stoichiometric reaction matrix restricted to the ions measured at the applicable evidence tier, $\lambda_1 = 0.01$, $\lambda_2 = 0.001$, and $\mathcal{Z}$ the non-negativity or saturation-index bound set; reactions requiring an unmeasured ion were penalised near-prohibitively so they could not be spuriously selected. Nothing in the M5 estimator or its calibration was re-fit on Ghanaian data: M6 is a transfer study.

Each inference unit was assigned one of four identifiability classes (non-identifiable, partially identifiable, equivalence-class, identifiable) and a 0-100 Mechanism Resolution Score (MRS) by projecting a nine-feature diagnostic vector (matrix-rank skill, coherence skill, bootstrap support stability, leave-one-ion held-out predictive skill, thermodynamic skill, and reconstruction statistics) through the frozen M5 calibration [@beven2001glue; @beven2006manifesto]. That calibration was fitted once, in the antecedent benchmark, on live-PHREEQC-generated synthetic scenarios spanning four hydrochemical archetypes with known reaction truth, and evaluated there on scenarios held out from fitting; it is applied here exactly as calibrated, with no parameter re-estimated on any Ghanaian sample, so that classification differences reported below reflect transfer to field data rather than local re-fitting. An evidence-lift gate then downgraded tracer-dependent process families (silicate, carbonate, evaporite, fluoride mobilisation) by one identifiability level whenever their corroborating tracer was absent or inconsistent (silica, strontium or saturation index for weathering; a deuterium-excess offset from the global meteoric water line for evaporite [@craig1961meteoric]; measured fluoride for fluoride mobilisation), so that a numerically confident fit could not be reported as resolved without independent chemical support. The fixed numeric thresholds defining tracer corroboration were predeclared operational thresholds rather than fitted to the Ghanaian data; they are not universal analytical reporting limits. A reviewer-requested low/default/high threshold analysis is reported separately from the locked primary analysis.

### Experiments, edges and robustness

![Figure 2. Transfer of the frozen M5 inverse model and Mechanism Resolution Score calibration onto three Ghana datasets through harmonisation, charge-balance screening, evidence tiers, candidate edges, transferred inference, evidence gates, robustness diagnostics and a limitation map.](figures/r_publication/figure2_workflow.png)

Six experiments implementing the workflow summarised in Figure 2 were executed on the harmonised data, in an order and with primary metrics fixed by a locked analysis plan before execution: dataset readiness and missingness; Northern Ghana seasonal-by-region transfer at Tier 4; a Tier 4-to-0 ablation quantifying identifiability loss as fluoride, isotopes and strontium/silica were withdrawn in turn; a flow-path-uncertainty experiment comparing three Hydrosheaf-generated candidate edge-construction strategies against each other (chemistry-similarity k-nearest-neighbours, geographic-nearest-neighbours, and a random/perturbed null set of matched size [@moracchini2025graphflow]); external transfer of the identical pipeline to Talensi and Lower Anayari at their native tiers, benchmarked against Northern Ghana re-run at matched tiers; and a limitation map contrasting conservative class-level reporting with single-best-fit reaction counts. Bootstrap resample counts for the support-stability diagnostic ranged from 8 to 48 depending on experiment, set for runtime rather than precision reasons; the count used for each reported statistic is stated in Supplementary Methods Section S4 and should be considered when comparing stability values across tables. Supporting robustness analyses (Supplementary Methods) tested whether the evidence gate alone drove apparent identifiability loss, validated the tier-collapse direction against synthetic data with known reaction truth, tested sensitivity to the transport-correction choice, diagnosed the Talensi charge imbalance, and screened candidate edges for competing no-flow explanations. A within-campaign one-step seasonal hold-forward benchmark, reused unmodified from the Milestone 7.2 (M7.2) supporting validation, compared persistence, an expanding mean-delta baseline and a HydroSheaf graph-ridge predictor.

### Reproducibility

The workflow was executed deterministically (fixed seed) via a single orchestrating script, with all derived tables and Nature-style figures generated in one reproducible run. Full derivations, algorithmic detail and secondary validation, sensitivity and robustness protocols are reported in the Supplementary Methods. An earlier stage of this study read a Northern Ghana workbook that additionally carried inferred `Dominant_Process`, `Aquifer_Evolution_Label` and `Graph_Edges` fields from a different, antecedent, non-independent analysis of the same boreholes; these have been removed entirely rather than retained as a concordance reference, so no claim in this study compares Hydrosheaf's inferred process labels or edge sets against them [@beven2001glue].

The frozen M5 inverse solver, MRS calibration and M7.2 hold-forward outputs on which this workflow depends are components of the same research programme as this study and are not, at the time of writing, independently peer-reviewed references. Analysis code, derived outputs, and access notes are maintained at `https://github.com/dabdul-wahab1988/Hydrosheaf`. The submitted version must also state the access conditions and source citations for the third-party raw measurements.

### Reviewer-requested robustness analyses

Recent Ghanaian studies use machine learning to predict groundwater-quality
outcomes in the Nabogo Basin and crystalline aquifer terrain
[@apogba2024nabogo; @abu2025ghana]. They complement, but do not duplicate,
this study: prediction of a quality index does not test whether a particular
reaction family remains identifiable when a diagnostic tracer is withheld.

The tier ladder is descended by removing fluoride from Tier 2 to Tier 1 and
then isotopes from Tier 1 to Tier 0; strontium and silica are removed earlier,
from Tier 3 to Tier 2. The combined evidence-gate sensitivity used low,
predeclared, and high corroboration profiles. Across these profiles, the Tier
2 non-identifiable fraction ranged only from 0.513 to 0.531 (predeclared
0.519), whereas Tier 3 ranged from 0.000 to 0.025 (predeclared 0.006). Thus,
the location and direction of the Tier 3-to-2 collapse are robust to these
reasonable threshold changes, although its exact magnitude remains conditional
on the operational gate.

The near-prohibitive penalty applied to reactions requiring unmeasured ions was
also varied from 20 to 10 and 50 in 40 fixed well pairs at Tiers 2 and 4. Mean
support Jaccard similarity was 0.990 for 10 versus 20 and 1.000 for 50 versus
20; mean maximum absolute extent change was 0.0091 and 0.0000 mmol/L,
respectively. The penalty therefore does not explain the principal tier
contrast over the tested range.

Bootstrap counts were originally set for runtime rather than precision. A
200-resample audit of 20 fixed Tier-4 pairs estimated the mean standard error
of stability at 0.0123 for B = 8, 0.0050 for B = 48, and 0.0025 for B = 200.
Comparisons using B = 8 should consequently be read as coarse diagnostics, not
high-precision estimates. Full unit-level and aggregate values are archived in
`results/m6_review_sensitivity.csv`.

The synthetic validation's exact-mineral F1 applies only to the fixed reaction
dictionary and simulated archetypes; family- and class-level recovery are the
transfer targets. Figure and table notes distinguish within-well wet-to-dry
pairs from chemistry-kNN candidate edges. The latter are unobserved screening
constructions, so a viable no-flow explanation remains a competing account of
every external-transfer contrast. Talensi's result cannot be attributed
causally to mining or any single process because charge-balance failure and
missing tracers make it an exploratory screening case only. Likewise, the
external datasets' smaller candidate pools can alter nearest-neighbour graph
structure independently of tracer coverage; prospective matched-size sampling
is needed to separate this sample-size confound from the tier effect.

Figure 1 and Table 1 establish the evidence ceiling that bounds every subsequent result. Only Northern Ghana reached the maximum M6 evidence tier (Tier 4); Lower Anayari was capped at Tier 2 by the absence of strontium and silica, and Talensi was capped at Tier 1 by the additional absence of fluoride (Table 2). An independent charge-balance audit, applied identically to all three datasets, classified Northern Ghana as 294 quantitative, 19 screening and 7 exploratory of 320 samples (median absolute charge-balance error 1.5%); no external quality-class label exists in the canonical source data to check this classification against. Lower Anayari returned 36 quantitative, 5 screening and 0 exploratory samples of 41 (median 3.1%). Talensi returned 0 quantitative, 5 screening and 58 exploratory samples of 63 (median approximately 30%), identifying it as a genuinely low-quality-control external set from the outset, before any reaction inference was attempted. Missingness was not monotonic with overall evidence tier: iron, for example, was recorded in both external datasets but absent from Northern Ghana, so evidence completeness had to be tracked per variable rather than assumed from a single dataset-level tier label.

Applied identically to the same candidate-edge sets used in the connectivity experiment below, a supplementary sensitivity screen for competing, non-flow explanations of sample similarity returned high scores throughout (Supplementary Fig. S11, Supplementary Table S9): chemistry-similarity edges scored a mean null score above 0.89 in every dataset, and 96-100% of edges in every dataset/edge-set combination exceeded the 0.5 competing-explanation screening threshold. Geographic-nearest edges scored only marginally lower than chemistry-similarity edges. Because chemical similarity is, by construction, also how the reaction-inference pipeline itself selects a dominant family, this result was treated as a caveat rather than a contradiction: it indicates that a constructed edge should not, by itself, be read as evidence of a physical flow connection, reinforcing rather than weakening the honesty boundary applied throughout this study.

### Northern Ghana seasonal and region-stratified transfer

![Figure 3. Northern Ghana wet/dry process-inference stability by region. (a) Identifiability class by region. (b) Dominant Cl-corrected seasonal process by region. (c) Mechanism Resolution Score by region; boxes show the interquartile range with the median line, whiskers extend to 1.5x the interquartile range, and points beyond are plotted individually.](figures/r_publication/figure3_ng_stability.png)

At full evidence (Tier 4), every one of the 160 Northern Ghana wells was classified partially identifiable, never identifiable and never non-identifiable (Figure 3), reproducing the conservative, class-level outcome previously found under the antecedent synthetic transfer. Mean MRS ranged narrowly from 70.6 (Upper West) to 71.2 (Northern Region), and mean bootstrap support stability from 0.965 to 0.976 across the four administrative regions (no independent aquifer-type classification exists for these boreholes), indicating that this conservative call was numerically stable and homogeneous rather than borderline or region-specific. The dominant Cl-corrected seasonal process was silicate weathering (114 of 160 wells), followed by redox transformation (18), cation exchange (16), agro-domestic nitrate loading (5), carbonate weathering (4) and evaporite dissolution (3), and silicate weathering was the modal process in every one of the four regions. No prior process-label concordance is reported: the only such reference available before this correction was a different, antecedent study's own inferred labels for the same boreholes, not independent ground truth, and it has been removed from this study rather than retained as a descriptive comparison (Methods).

Tier ablation produced the study's pivotal result (Figure 4, Table 3). Descending from Tier 4 to Tier 3 (removing only the saturation-index lifter) left identifiability essentially unchanged (0% to 0.6% non-identifiable; mean MRS 71.0 to 70.7). Descending one further step, from Tier 3 to Tier 2 (removing strontium and silica), sent 51.9% of wells to non-identifiable while mean MRS barely moved (70.7 to 70.7). Removing fluoride (Tier 1) and then isotopes and all remaining lifters (Tier 0) left the non-identifiable fraction close to this level (51.9% and 60.0% respectively) and lowered mean score only modestly (to 68.6 and 68.4). Strontium and silica were therefore the load-bearing corroborating measurements for resolving silicate weathering, and their removal was the single largest driver of identifiability loss observed anywhere in this study. Reaction-family labels, in contrast, changed relative to the Tier 4 reference for a constant 35.6% of wells at every ablated tier from Tier 3 down to Tier 0, showing that family-level attribution and identifiability-class collapse are governed by different features of the evidence and do not move together. Across every tier, mean MRS changed by at most 2.6 points while the non-identifiable fraction moved by up to 51 percentage points between Tier 3 and Tier 2, and by up to 60 percentage points across the full Tier 4 to Tier 0 range, so numerical fit quality alone would not have revealed this loss of information.

These tier-ablation figures use within-well seasonal wet-to-dry residuals as the inference unit throughout, which matters for comparison with the external-transfer results below: the Northern Ghana reference values reported there are instead built from chemistry-similarity edges among dry-season samples only, a different inference-unit construction that is not directly comparable tier-for-tier to the seasonal-pair figures above, and the two should not be read as reproducing one another at nominally matching tiers.

### Edge and flow-path uncertainty

Comparing three Hydrosheaf-generated candidate edge-construction strategies against each other showed that inferred process-*network* composition was sensitive to the assumed connectivity: total variation distance from the chemistry-similarity reference set was 0.12 for geographic-nearest edges and 0.05 for the random/perturbed null set (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S5). Per-edge identifiability, however, was effectively edge-invariant: 99.3-100% of edges were classified partially identifiable and mean MRS ranged only 72.6-73.3 across all three edge sets. Connectivity assumptions therefore affected which process network was inferred at the network level, but not whether a given point-to-point reaction inference was resolvable, isolating exactly where an uncertain flow-path assumption does and does not matter.

**Extended Data Fig. 3.** Field non-identifiable fraction with the evidence-lift gate on versus off, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, alongside gate-independent structural diagnostics and transport-correction sensitivity. Retained as an explicit anti-circularity diagnostic.

Three supporting analyses tested whether the tier-ablation result above was an artefact of the evidence-lift gate rather than a real information effect. First, repeating the tier ablation with the gate switched off showed that the field data alone, without the gate, never returned a non-identifiable classification at any tier (0% throughout; Extended Data Fig. 3), while a gate-independent structural diagnostic on an extended reaction-basis model showed matrix rank rising from 8 to 11 and silicate-versus-non-silicate coherence falling from 0.707 to 0.500 as strontium and silica were added, confirming that the direction of the tier effect is real even though its reported magnitude is a property of the conservative gate. Second, a synthetic-well validation with known reaction truth (Extended Data Fig. 2) showed that adding strontium and silica genuinely improved recovery where theory predicts (cation-exchange family accuracy 0.45 to 0.66; carbonate and silicate family accuracy approximately 1.00 and 0.98 respectively), while exact-mineral-level recovery remained limited at every tier (F1 approximately 0.49-0.74), which is the quantitative justification for reporting at the class/family level throughout this study; the same synthetic wells spanned the full range of true identifiability class (35% identifiable, 30% partially identifiable, 23% non-identifiable, 12% equivalence-class). Because this is a synthetic discrimination check rather than field truth, it shows that the classifier can express all four classes but does not establish that Northern Ghana is hydrogeochemically homogeneous or that its analytical quality is uniformly high. Third, comparing three transport-correction treatments showed that omitting a correction altogether was degenerate (93% of wells collapsed to a spurious evapoconcentration signal), indicating that some transport correction is necessary. The two principled corrections, chloride-conservative and per-region recharge-endmember, agreed on the dominant reactive family for only 64.4% of wells, meaning more than a third of wells received a different dominant-family call depending on which correction was used; both corrections nonetheless identified an overall silicate-weathering-dominated network at the aggregate level (71% and 70% of wells respectively), so the network-level qualitative picture is similar across the two treatments even though well-level attribution is not, and this well-level sensitivity to the correction choice should be read as a genuine, only partially resolved source of uncertainty rather than as evidence of full correction-choice invariance. A targeted diagnosis of the Talensi charge imbalance (median -29%, a persistent 2.1-2.12 meq/L anion excess dominated by bicarbonate) showed that reassigning alkalinity from bicarbonate to calcium-carbonate units worsened rather than improved the balance, ruling out a unit-reporting error. The remaining explanations include an unmeasured cation pool or cation under-recovery, but the available data cannot identify the cause or attribute it specifically to artisanal-mining activity.

## Discussion

### What Hydrosheaf adds beyond conventional chemistry interpretation

Conventional hydrochemical interpretation of these three settings would typically proceed from Gibbs and Piper-type diagrams and facies classification to a single narrative account of dominant process [@gibbs1970mechanisms; @piper1944graphic] (Supplementary Fig. S1). That approach would have reported a broadly similar headline story for Northern Ghana, silicate weathering dominant across the sampled region, with cation exchange, redox and nitrate loading as secondary processes, because the underlying chemistry is genuinely dominated by these processes.

What it would not have reported is the tier ablation's central finding: that a numerically confident, well-fitting reaction estimate persisted essentially unchanged in apparent quality as strontium and silica were withdrawn, even though the fraction of wells for which silicate weathering could be independently corroborated collapsed from effectively complete to just under half. That collapse is itself the output of a designed conservatism prior, the evidence-lift gate, rather than a direct empirical measurement; its direction, but not its exact numerical magnitude, was independently validated against synthetic data with known truth. A workflow that reports goodness-of-fit alongside an explicit, tracer-conditioned identifiability class, and that discloses the gate as a conservatism prior rather than an intrinsic property of the data, makes this gap visible; one that reports fit quality alone does not. That is the specific, demonstrated value added here, and it is a value that shows up only once the workflow is stress-tested against withdrawn evidence rather than evaluated on the best-available dataset alone.

### Where it reduces overinterpretation and where it cannot resolve equifinality

The synthetic ground-truth validation showed that exact-mineral identification remained limited (F1 approximately 0.5-0.7) even at the richest evidence tier, while family- and class-level recovery was substantially better and improved specifically where added tracers were mechanistically relevant. This is consistent with the broader equifinality literature's observation that non-uniqueness in reaction-path and mass-balance inference is a structural property of the problem, not a defect specific to this workflow or this dataset [@beven2001glue; @beven2006manifesto]. Reporting at the class or family level, and admitting on average 7.1 alternative reactions where a single best fit would commit to 5.4, is therefore not a hedge but a calibrated response to a genuine, quantified limit: the exact reaction cannot be identified from this evidence, in this system, regardless of which inference method is used.

Where the workflow adds discipline is in making that limit explicit and dataset-specific rather than leaving it implicit in an unstated single answer. It cannot, and does not claim to, resolve the underlying non-uniqueness; the edge-uncertainty experiment showed the same pattern at the network level, where the assumed connectivity changed which process network was inferred without changing whether any individual point-to-point inference was itself resolvable.

### Relationship to the antecedent Northern Ghana field application

The antecedent synthetic benchmark from which the inverse solver and Mechanism Resolution Score calibration are frozen also included its own chemistry-only field application to Northern Ghana wet/dry well pairs, and the population analysed there overlaps substantially with the 160-well Northern Ghana dataset used for the seasonal transfer experiment reported here. This overlap is stated explicitly rather than left implicit. What this study adds beyond that prior application is the full Tier 4 evidence stack (isotopes, fluoride, strontium/silica, and saturation indices computed independently by PHREEQC from the same measured chemistry) rather than majors alone, the region-stratified breakdown, and, more substantially, the four additional experiments that the prior application did not include: the tier-by-tier ablation that produces the paper's central fit-quality-versus-identifiability finding, the edge-construction sensitivity analysis, transfer to two external datasets, and the synthesised limitation map. The seasonal-transfer experiment (E2) on its own, considered in isolation from these four additional experiments, would not constitute a sufficient independent contribution, and it is not presented as one; it functions here as the Tier 4 reference point against which the tier-ablation, edge-uncertainty and external-transfer experiments are all benchmarked.

### Monitoring implications: the next-best measurement per dataset

Because the tier-ablation and external-transfer results isolate strontium and silica as the specific measurements whose absence drives Lower Anayari's near-total non-identifiability and a substantial share of Northern Ghana's tier-3-to-tier-2 collapse, the practical implication is concrete rather than generic: for a silicate-dominated, data-limited Ghanaian aquifer, adding strontium and silica to an otherwise standard major-ion-plus-isotope panel is a testable candidate for reducing ambiguity. The present comparison does not establish that adding these tracers is superior to adding wells, because panel completeness and dataset sample size are confounded in the external-transfer analysis. Strontium and silica have long been used as discriminants between silicate- and carbonate-derived solutes in hydrogeochemistry; the contribution here is not the discovery that these tracers matter, which is established domain knowledge, but a dataset-specific, quantified demonstration of how much identifiability is lost when they are absent and a transferable illustration of how the same tier-ablation logic could be applied to price the value of any other candidate measurement. Fluoride mobilisation, a documented regional concern given widespread geogenic fluoride occurrence in Ghanaian and wider West African groundwater [@podgorski2022fluoride; @onipe2020fluoride], and agro-domestic nitrate loading [@tredoux2006nitrate] both remained majors-corroborable and comparatively stable across tiers in this study, so the measurement-value argument here is specific to weathering-family resolution and should not be generalised to every process class without re-running the same tier-ablation logic for the process of interest.

### Interpretation and honesty boundary of the field application

This study evaluated field transfer of a chemistry-based reaction-inference audit; it did not, and could not, independently validate groundwater residence time, exact directed flow paths, or the true reaction mechanism at any well, consistent with the boundary stated in Study design. An earlier stage of this study compared Hydrosheaf's inferred process labels against a different, antecedent study's own prior process labels for the same boreholes; because those labels are themselves the non-independent output of another analysis rather than measured or externally validated quantities, that comparison has been removed rather than reported, and no claim here rests on agreement or disagreement with them. The within-campaign seasonal hold-forward result likewise supports a narrow claim about chemistry predictability and says nothing about residence time or topology. The appropriate reading of these results is that an identifiability-aware workflow can transfer usefully to real, uneven African aquifer data as a conservative audit of what current evidence supports, while remaining explicit that it is not, and does not claim to be, a substitute for independent hydraulic, isotopic-age or tracer-based validation of flow and residence time.

Five limits follow directly and bound the scope of any generalisation from these results. First, only one of the three datasets reached the tier at which strontium/silica-based corroboration was possible, so the tier-ablation result, while internally validated against known synthetic truth, has been demonstrated as a within-Ghana transfer and not yet as a transfer across substantially different lithological provinces elsewhere in West Africa. Second, the within-campaign hold-forward benchmark evaluated one dry season following one wet season at one set of wells; it does not establish predictive skill across multiple years or across a genuine change in climatic or abstraction regime. Third, every robustness and validation analysis reported here was performed on the same three datasets used for the headline results, so the next natural test of this workflow is prospective application to an independently collected dataset with strontium, silica and repeat seasonal sampling by design, which would allow the next-best-measurement recommendation itself to be evaluated rather than only motivated.

Fourth, the numeric thresholds defining tracer corroboration in the evidence-lift gate are operational choices rather than universal analytical reporting limits. The threshold-profile sensitivity analysis showed only a narrow change in Tier 2 non-identifiability (51.3-53.1%) and Tier 3 non-identifiability (0.0-2.5%) across the tested profiles, but the precise collapse magnitude remains conditional on this tested range. Fifth, the external-transfer comparison assumes that chemistry-similarity edge networks built within Talensi's 63 and Lower Anayari's 41 samples are structurally comparable to the Northern Ghana reference network built within a much larger pool of 320 samples; nearest-neighbour graphs constructed within smaller pools are likely to differ structurally from those built within larger ones independent of any tracer-availability effect, and this sample-size-dependent confound has not been separated from the tier effect this study set out to isolate.

## Conclusion

Transferring an identifiability-aware sparse inverse reaction-inference workflow, together with its frozen reliability calibration, onto three real Ghanaian aquifer datasets showed that it degrades gracefully, honestly and largely reproducibly as field evidence is withdrawn, but not without limit. Dataset readiness varied sharply and predictably with tracer coverage and charge-balance quality across the three settings. Within the best-resourced dataset, seasonal and region-stratified transfer was stable, with every well reported conservatively as partially identifiable rather than over-resolved. Diagnostic robustness testing identified strontium and silica, not isotopes or fluoride, as the specific measurements whose removal drove the largest, sharpest loss of identifiability, while numerical fit quality remained essentially unchanged throughout, showing that fit alone cannot be trusted to reveal this loss. Connectivity assumptions changed which process network was inferred without changing whether individual point-to-point reactions were themselves resolvable. External transfer to two genuinely sparse datasets showed substantially higher non-identifiability than matched-tier Northern Ghana references, with the same strontium/silica mechanism responsible for the most severe case. Across all datasets, silicate and carbonate weathering proved harder to resolve without corroborating tracers than majors-corroborable processes such as nitrate loading, redox transformation and cation exchange. Hydrosheaf is therefore useful, in this application, as a conservative, evidence-gated field-transfer framework that states which process interpretations survive sparse data, which collapse, and which single measurement would most reduce ambiguity at a given site - and it is not, and does not claim to be, a substitute for independent validation of true reactions, flow paths or groundwater ages in these aquifers.
