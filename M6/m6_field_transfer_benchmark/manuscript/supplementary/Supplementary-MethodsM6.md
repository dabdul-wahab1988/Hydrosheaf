# Supplementary Methods

This section provides the technically complete, reproducibility-level description of the M6 field-transfer workflow. The main-text Methods section describes only the core workflow, primary modelling choices and principal validation design; all derivations, implementation-sensitive settings, and secondary validation, sensitivity, ablation and robustness procedures are described here in full.

### S1. Data harmonisation, unit conversion and ion-name crosswalk

Three source files were harmonised into a common schema of eleven analytes (Ca, Mg, Na, K, HCO3, Cl, SO4, NO3, F, Fe, PO4), two stable isotopes (delta18O, delta2H), two weathering tracers (Sr, SiO2, retained in mg/L), five saturation indices (calcite, dolomite, gypsum, halite, fluorite) and coordinate fields. The Northern Ghana workbook contributed separate `Dry` and `Wet` sheets, concatenated with a `Season` tag; each sheet carries its own major ions, isotopes, Sr, SiO2, coordinates, Well_ID, Region and District directly, with no separate metadata worksheet to merge. This raw workbook carries no independent aquifer-type, geology-group, lithology or land-use classification for these boreholes. Talensi and Lower Anayari were supplied as flat CSV files with dataset-specific column names, mapped explicitly to the common ion crosswalk (for example, Talensi's `HCO3`, `Na`, `K`, `Ca`, `Mg`, `Cl`, `SO4`, `NO3` columns and Lower Anayari's additional `F` column). All ion concentrations were converted from mg/L to mmol/L by division by molar mass; Fe was harmonised identically where present and set to missing otherwise. Talensi longitudes were recorded in the source file as a positive magnitude in degrees west and were converted to signed WGS84 longitude (multiplied by -1) so that plotted positions fall within Ghana rather than east of the Greenwich meridian. Lower Anayari coordinates were taken directly from the source `X coordinate`/`Y coordinate` fields. Neither external dataset carried a season indicator; both were treated as single-campaign and assigned `season = "unknown"`.

### S2. Independent charge-balance error calculation and QC-tier classification

Charge-balance error (CBE) was recomputed independently from the harmonised mmol/L ion concentrations rather than taken from any source-reported value, so that data quality could be audited rather than assumed:

<!-- EQ:EQ-S1 -->
$$
\mathrm{CBE}~(\%) = 100 \times \frac{\sum_c z_c [c] - \sum_a |z_a| [a]}{\sum_c z_c [c] + \sum_a |z_a| [a]}
$$

where $[c]$ and $[a]$ are cation and anion concentrations in mmol/L and $z$ their signed charge (Ca +2, Mg +2, Na +1, K +1; HCO3 -1, Cl -1, SO4 -2, NO3 -1, F -1). Each sample was assigned to a quality class by $|\mathrm{CBE}|$: quantitative ($\leq 5\%$), screening ($5$-$10\%$), or exploratory ($>10\%$) [@fritz1994charge]. Applied to Northern Ghana, the independent CBE classified 294 quantitative, 19 screening and 7 exploratory samples of 320 (median $|\mathrm{CBE}| = 1.5\%$); no external quality-class field exists in the canonical source data to compare this classification against. Lower Anayari returned 36 quantitative, 5 screening and 0 exploratory samples of 41 (median $|\mathrm{CBE}| = 3.1\%$). Talensi returned 0 quantitative, 5 screening and 58 exploratory samples of 63 (median $|\mathrm{CBE}| \approx 30\%$), identifying it as a genuinely low-quality-control external set dominated by a persistent anion excess (Section S10). Primary Northern Ghana analyses used only the 294 quantitative records; screening and exploratory records entered sensitivity reporting only.

### S3. Evidence-tier ladder and inference-unit definitions

Five nested evidence tiers were defined to make the experimental design explicit rather than incidental. Tier 0 comprised the eight major ions (Ca, Mg, Na, K, HCO3, Cl, SO4, NO3), which formed the linear reaction-basis panel at every tier. Tier 1 added stable isotopes (delta18O, delta2H) as an evidence lifter without extending the linear panel. Tier 2 added fluoride to the linear panel, enabling fluorite/apatite-type reactions. Tier 3 added strontium and silica as weathering-corroboration lifters. Tier 4 added saturation indices for calcite, dolomite, gypsum, halite and fluorite, computed independently by live PHREEQC from each borehole or sample's own measured pH, temperature and major-ion panel rather than supplied by any source workbook or file. Native ion-panel tier ceilings were Tier 4 for Northern Ghana, Tier 2 for Lower Anayari (no Sr/SiO2, no season) and Tier 1 for Talensi (no F/Sr/SiO2, no season), fixed by data availability. Saturation-index availability does not follow this same ceiling: because saturation indices require only pH, temperature and major ions rather than strontium or silica, they were computed for Talensi and Lower Anayari exactly as for Northern Ghana and made available as an evidence-lift-gate lifter (Section S5) at every tier for both external datasets, including at their native Tier 1/Tier 2 ion panels; their exclusion from the nested Tier 0-4 ladder above reflects the ladder's own construction (Tier 4 is defined as Tier 3 plus saturation indices) rather than a data ceiling on saturation indices specifically (Methods, Results). No independent aquifer-type, geology-group or lithology classification exists for any of the three datasets at the individual-well level. The inference unit was a directed concentration change: for Northern Ghana, the within-well wet-to-dry seasonal pair (Section S11 defines pairing); for the edge-uncertainty and external-transfer experiments, the concentration difference along a candidate directed edge between two samples (Section S6).

### S4. Sparse inverse reaction estimator and transferred identifiability classification

The reactive residual $\mathbf{r}$ for an inference unit (Section S3) was decomposed into reaction extents using the frozen Milestone 5 (M5) FISTA-based sparse-regression estimator, applied to M6 data without any re-fitting:

<!-- EQ:EQ-S2 -->
$$
\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{z} \,\in\, \mathcal{Z}}
\; \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{r} \rVert_2^2
+ \lambda_1 \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_1
+ \lambda_2 \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert_2^2,
\qquad \lambda_1 = 0.01,\ \lambda_2 = 0.001
$$

with $\mathbf{A}$ the stoichiometric matrix of the 16-reaction dictionary (silicate weathering via albite/K-feldspar/biotite/anorthite, carbonate weathering via calcite/dolomite, evaporite dissolution via halite/gypsum, cation exchange, fluoride/apatite mobilisation, redox and nitrate-source reactions) restricted to ions measured at the applicable tier, and $\mathcal{Z}$ either the non-negative orthant or, where saturation indices were available, thermodynamic bounds derived from the upstream calcite/dolomite/gypsum/halite/fluorite indices. Reactions requiring an ion absent from the current panel received a penalty scale of 20 (versus 1 for eligible reactions), which is large enough, relative to the achievable residual magnitudes, to exclude them from the selected support without altering the optimisation of eligible reactions.

Each fitted unit was reduced to a nine-feature diagnostic vector: rank skill (achieved matrix rank over the minimum of panel size and equivalence-class count), coherence skill (one minus the class-collapsed mutual coherence), bootstrap support stability, held-out predictive skill, thermodynamic skill (one minus the normalised count of thermodynamic-bound violations), reconstruction RMSE, held-out RMSE, panel size, and the fitted L1 norm. Support stability was the mean Jaccard similarity between the base support and the support recovered from resamples of the residual perturbed by 5% multiplicative analytical noise:

<!-- EQ:EQ-S4 -->
$$
\text{stability} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} J\!\left(S_0,\, S_b\right), \qquad
J(S_0, S_b) = \frac{|S_0 \cap S_b|}{|S_0 \cup S_b|}
$$

with $B \in \{8, 12, 16, 48\}$ depending on experiment (fewer resamples were used for the larger edge-based experiments to control runtime) and $S_0$, $S_b$ the base and resampled reaction supports [@meinshausen2010stability]. Held-out predictive skill used a leave-one-ion refit: each measured ion was withheld, the remaining ions were refit, and the predicted change in the withheld ion was compared with its observed residual; this was computed only for panels with more than three ions.

The nine-feature vector was standardised by the frozen M5 feature means and scales, prepended with an intercept, and projected through the frozen class-coefficient matrix (argmax over four classes: non-identifiable, partially identifiable, equivalence-class, identifiable) and the frozen reliability-coefficient vector, clipped to [0, 100], to give the Mechanism Resolution Score (MRS). If the argmax class was "identifiable" but the selected support contained a member of an ambiguous equivalence class (more than one reaction sharing the same class label), the reported class was downgraded to "equivalence-class". No feature mean, scale, class coefficient or reliability coefficient was altered from the M5 calibration; this is the defining property of a transfer study rather than a re-calibration [@beven2001glue; @beven2006manifesto].

### S5. Evidence-lift gate and tracer-corroboration rules

A base identifiability class from Section S4 could still overstate what an inference unit's dominant process family had actually demonstrated, because a numerically well-fitted reaction can be selected even when its corroborating tracer is absent. An evidence-lift gate therefore checked, for the dominant reaction family only, whether an independent tracer corroborated it, and downgraded the reported class by one level (identifiable to equivalence-class, equivalence-class to partially identifiable, partially identifiable to non-identifiable) whenever it did not:

- **Silicate weathering:** corroborated when SiO2 was measured and $\geq 10$ mg/L.
- **Carbonate weathering:** corroborated when a calcite saturation index was available, or when Sr was measured and $\geq 0.10$ mg/L.
- **Evapoconcentration:** corroborated when both isotopes were measured and the sample fell close to the global meteoric water line, operationalised as a deuterium-excess condition referenced to the line's canonical slope of 8 [@craig1961meteoric]:

<!-- EQ:EQ-S3 -->
$$
\delta^2\mathrm{H} - 8\,\delta^{18}\mathrm{O} < 8
$$

- **Fluoride mobilisation:** corroborated when F was measured and $> 0.05$ mmol/L.

Process families inferable from major ions alone (cation exchange, agro-domestic nitrate loading, redox transformation) were not tracer-gated and returned corroborated by construction. The gate was applied identically with `apply_gate=True` in every reported experiment; a parallel ungated run (Section S7) quantified how much of the apparent identifiability loss under tier ablation was attributable to the gate itself rather than to the underlying reaction-basis structure.

### S6. Candidate edge-set construction algorithms

Three edge-construction strategies, all generated internally by Hydrosheaf from the harmonised data rather than imported, were compared for the Northern Ghana flow-path-uncertainty experiment, and chemistry-similarity edges were reused for the external-transfer experiment. An earlier revision of this workflow also compared against a fourth, "provided graph" edge set imported from a retired antecedent-study workbook; that set has been removed rather than substituted (Methods). (1) **Chemistry-kNN**: for each sample, the $k=3$ nearest neighbours by Euclidean distance in standardised major-ion space (eight ions, mean-imputed, z-scored), with edge direction assigned from the lower- to the higher-ionic-load sample. (2) **Geographic-nearest**: identical neighbour selection using Euclidean distance in latitude/longitude, again $k=3$, with the same load-based direction rule. (3) **Random/perturbed**: a uniformly random sample of unordered pairs, drawn without replacement and matched in size to the chemistry-kNN set, used as a null comparator [@moracchini2025graphflow]. For the main edge experiment, each edge set was capped at 140 edges after shuffling with the fixed seed. For external transfer, chemistry-kNN edges ($k=3$) were constructed independently within Talensi and within Lower Anayari, since neither dataset provided geographic coordinates suitable for a comparable geographic construction at native tier.

### S7. Robustness and circularity diagnostics (evidence-gate on/off, structural diagnostics, bootstrap confidence intervals)

Because the evidence-lift gate (Section S5) could in principle manufacture the appearance of identifiability loss under tier ablation, the Northern Ghana tier ablation was re-run recording both the gated class (Section S5) and the ungated base class (Section S4) for every well at every tier. In parallel, an extended reaction-basis model was constructed in which Sr and SiO2 were promoted from evidence lifters to genuine reaction-basis species (silicate weathering releases SiO2; carbonate and gypsum dissolution release Sr), so that their diagnostic value could be assessed by gate-independent linear-algebra diagnostics: matrix rank, nullity, number of equivalence classes, and silicate-versus-non-silicate mutual coherence, computed once per tier-equivalent panel (majors; majors+F; majors+F+Sr+SiO2). The gated non-identifiable fraction at each tier was additionally reported with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval obtained by resampling wells with replacement ($B = 400$). This design separated three questions that a single ablation curve cannot, consistent with a structural-uncertainty framing in which model output and model structure are diagnosed separately [@neuman2003bma]: whether the gate alone produces non-identifiable calls (it does; the ungated classifier never returned non-identifiable on field data), whether the direction of the tier effect is real (it is, evidenced by structural rank rising and silicate coherence falling as Sr/SiO2 were added), and how large the gate's conservatism is once its direction is validated (Section S8).

### S8. Synthetic ground-truth validation protocol

Because field data carry no independent reaction truth, a synthetic-well generator forward-simulated 360 wells with known reactions and known extents, so that recovery could be scored against ground truth rather than against the gate's own output. Each well was assigned an archetype (silicate, carbonate, evaporite, ion-exchange, nitrate, or mixed, drawn with fixed probabilities), a random subset of one to three reactions from the archetype's candidate pool, and extents drawn uniformly from [0.25, 1.5]. Reaction stoichiometry was applied to a dilute recharge-endmember composition (Ca 0.30, Mg 0.15, Na 0.35, K 0.05, HCO3 1.0, Cl 0.20, SO4 0.08, NO3 0.05, F 0.005, SiO2 0.15, Sr 0.002 mmol/L) and thermodynamic bounds consistent with the saturation-index framework used for inverse geochemical calculations [@parkhurst2013phreeqc], the mixture was evaporatively concentrated by a factor drawn uniformly from [1.0, 2.0], and 3% multiplicative analytical noise was added. The identical inference pipeline (Sections S3-S4) was then applied at three evidence panels (majors only; majors+F; majors+F+Sr+SiO2), using a chloride-estimated evaporation factor consistent with the field pipeline's transport correction, and recovery was scored against the known active-reaction set by exact-phase precision/recall/F1, class-level F1 (using the same equivalence classes as the field pipeline), family-level F1, and dominant-family accuracy (by summed absolute extent). A true identifiability class was also assigned from the known truth and the recovered support, independently of the M6 evidence gate, to test whether the framework could discriminate across a genuinely varying information state.

### S9. Transport-correction sensitivity protocol

Three treatments of the Northern Ghana within-well seasonal residual were compared: no transport correction ($f=1$); the field-default Cl-conservative correction (Section S3 of the main Methods, Equation EQ-1), consistent with a conservative-tracer end-member framing [@christophersen1992emma]; and a per-region recharge-endmember correction, in which the source composition for every well in a region was replaced by that region's minimum-ionic-load sample (a proxy for the least-evolved, most-recharge-like water present) while the transport factor was still estimated from chloride. Dominant reaction family was compared across treatments, with the Cl-conservative treatment used as the reference for agreement statistics, and mean MRS was compared to check that the identifiability score was not itself an artefact of the correction choice.

### S10. Talensi charge-balance diagnosis

Because Talensi's charge-balance imbalance (Section S2) was large enough to restrict its usable claim strength, its source was diagnosed rather than merely reported. Median cation and anion equivalents were compared directly, the bicarbonate share of total anion equivalents was computed, and a counter-test recomputed the balance under the alternative hypothesis that alkalinity had been reported as CaCO3 rather than as HCO3 (a common reporting convention that, if applicable, would require rescaling by the ratio of the CaCO3 and HCO3 equivalent weights, 61.02/50.04). The counter-test worsened rather than improved the imbalance, ruling out an alkalinity-unit misassignment as the explanation. The persistent anion excess, concentrated in bicarbonate, was therefore attributed to an unmeasured cation pool (no trace-metal cations were reported for this artisanal-mining catchment) or to cation under-recovery in the source analyses, and Talensi was retained as a screening-only, charge-balance-limited vignette rather than as a quantitative transfer result.

### S11. Within-campaign seasonal hold-forward benchmark (reused M7.2 protocol)

A one-step, within-campaign hold-forward benchmark was reused unmodified from the Milestone 7.2 (M7.2) supporting validation to test whether wet-to-dry chemistry change was predictable at all, independently of the reaction-inference pipeline above. The evaluation used 140 Northern Ghana wells with complete quantitative wet and dry records (independently screened by charge-balance error, |CBE| <= 5%), revealed in 20 fixed, disclosed, seeded pseudo-random batches: the canonical field data record one dry-season (March 2025) and one wet-season (August 2025) observation per well with no intra-season sampling-date field, so a real chronological issue sequence cannot be reconstructed, and the batch order is stated as arbitrary rather than implied to be a true sampling sequence. Dry-season features were always available before the corresponding wet-season label was issued for each batch, and no not-yet-revealed batch's wet-season label was used at prediction time. Three predictors were compared: persistence (dry equals wet), an expanding wet-to-dry mean-delta model fitted on previously revealed batches, and a HydroSheaf graph-ridge predictor (pre-registered ridge penalty $\alpha = 10$, minimum 16 training samples) using a self-generated, head-directed nearest-neighbour upstream-chemistry feature (no externally supplied edge graph is used). Performance was reported as mean absolute error, root-mean-square error and bias on the log1p-transformed concentration scale, together with empirical coverage of the nominal 90% predictive interval, both overall and by ion. A paired well-block bootstrap contrasted graph-ridge against persistence and against the expanding mean-delta baseline, resampling by well to respect within-well correlation, and reported the mean paired difference with a 95% confidence interval. This benchmark supports a claim about within-campaign chemistry predictability under a disclosed arbitrary revelation order only; it does not evaluate residence time, flow-path topology, reaction identity, or a true temporal sequence, and coordinates remained spatially masked throughout. The design follows the general logic, evaluating predictive skill under progressively withdrawn information, of the Predictions in Ungauged Basins literature, though that literature addresses streamflow rather than hydrogeochemical prediction [@sivapalan2003pub; @hrachowitz2013pub].

### S12. Competing no-flow (null-model) sensitivity protocol

A supplementary sensitivity analysis scored every candidate edge from Section S6 on six competing, non-flow explanations for why two samples might resemble each other even absent a physical connection: chemical similarity, shared lithology (explicit and inferred), isotopic proximity, shared recharge signal, shared anthropogenic signature, and spatial autocorrelation. Each component score was combined into a single null score per edge using HydroSheaf's existing evidence-classification logic. Scores above 0.5 were flagged as a competing no-flow explanation and scores above 0.8 as a dominant no-flow explanation; both thresholds are pre-existing HydroSheaf screening conventions and were not fitted to M6 data, and neither should be read as a calibrated probability that a given edge is a true no-flow artefact. This analysis is a sensitivity layer over the edge-uncertainty experiment (Section S6) and does not alter any locked field-transfer result; it restricts how confidently a provided or constructed edge may be read as evidence of physical connectivity.

### S13. Uncertainty-metric definitions

Six uncertainty and reliability metrics were used throughout and are summarised for reference: support stability (mean Jaccard similarity of the selected reaction support across analytical-noise bootstrap resamples, 0-1, higher indicates more stable selection); the Mechanism Resolution Score (0-100 reliability score from the frozen M5 calibration); held-out-ion RMSE (leave-one-ion predictive error of the concentration change); reactive RMSE (residual after transport correction and inversion); identifiability class (the four-level non/partially/equivalence-class/identifiable classification, transferred classifier plus evidence-lift gate); and evidence corroboration (a Boolean flag for whether the dominant process family was supported by an available, consistent tracer). ### S14. Removal of borrowed antecedent-study fields

An earlier revision of this workflow read a Northern Ghana workbook (`Aquifers_Dataset_Mendeley.xlsx`) that, beyond the raw measured chemistry, additionally carried `Dominant_Process`, `Aquifer_Evolution_Label`, `Graph_Edges` and aquifer/geology/lithology/land-use metadata fields. Those fields were themselves the inferred output of a different, antecedent, non-independent analysis of the same boreholes ("Graph-inverted Ghanaian aquifers under aridification"), not measured or independently validated quantities, and that workbook is not part of this project's own field data: it does not exist in the canonical data directory used throughout this study. Reusing it would have imported another study's own classifications and its own precomputed connectivity graph into what is meant to be an independent Hydrosheaf transfer test. Those fields, and the workbook that supplied them, have therefore been removed from this workflow entirely rather than retained as a concordance reference: no experiment, table or figure in this study compares a Hydrosheaf-inferred dominant family or edge set against them, and Northern Ghana chemistry throughout comes only from the canonical raw `NorthernGhana.xlsx` workbook's `Dry`/`Wet` sheets. This applies equally to the within-campaign hold-forward benchmark (Section S11), whose predictive skill is a statement about chemistry predictability, not about residence time, topology or reaction identity, and to the null-model sensitivity analysis (Section S12), whose thresholds are screening flags rather than calibrated field probabilities.

### S15. Reproducibility protocol and computational environment

The complete field-transfer analysis was executed by a single orchestrating script that ran the six experiments in Section S3-S6/main Methods in a fixed order, followed by the supporting robustness, synthetic-validation, transport-sensitivity, Talensi-diagnosis and null-sensitivity analyses described above, and finally the table- and figure-generation scripts. Every stochastic step (bootstrap resampling in Sections S4 and S7, edge perturbation in Section S6, synthetic-well generation in Section S8) used a single fixed random seed, and no output depended on wall-clock or system time. The computational environment was Python 3.14.6 with numpy 2.4.6 and pandas 2.3.3; the inverse solver and Mechanism Resolution Score calibration were the frozen M5 artefacts described in the main Methods and Section S4, applied without modification. Figures were rendered in R using a shared Nature-portfolio theme, at 300 dpi with vector PDF, 600-dpi PNG and LZW-compressed TIFF outputs for every main, Extended Data and supplementary figure.
